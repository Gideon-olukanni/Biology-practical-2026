What is WAEC Biology Practical?

Biology Paper 3 (Alternative A) tests whether you can observe, identify, draw, compare, and explain biological specimens in a real exam setting. It is the most mark-intensive Biology paper — 80 marks — and the easiest to score high on if you prepare correctly.

80

Total Marks

3 compulsory questions. Every mark counts toward your final grade.

2hrs

Duration

Roughly 40 minutes per question. Manage your time from question 1.

3

Questions

Each question focuses on a different biological theme or group of specimens.

–1

Penalty

One mark deducted if you write comparisons in prose instead of a table.

What Examiners Test

  • Identify the specimen by correct name (common and/or scientific)
  • List observable/external features (what you can see, touch, feel)
  • State functions of structures
  • Compare two specimens in a table
  • Draw and label a biological diagram
  • State adaptive features with explanations
  • State economic importance
  • Classify: Kingdom → Phylum → Class
  • Describe ecological relationships (parasitism, mutualism, etc.)
  • Food tests and expected results

🔑 Chief Examiner's Secret

WAEC examiners award marks for content points, not for long paragraphs. One clear sentence per point is better than four vague sentences. Every observable feature must be from direct observation — describe what you see on the specimen, not what your textbook says.

The WAEC Question Formula

Every WAEC Biology practical question follows a predictable structure. Once you know the formula, no question will surprise you.

Step 1 — Identification

"Identify specimen A / State the common name of specimen B."
1–2 marks

Step 2 — Observable Features

"State THREE observable features of specimen A."
3 marks (1 per point)

Step 3 — Functions / Uses

"State TWO functions of the structure labelled X" or "State TWO economic importances."
2–4 marks

Step 4 — Classification

"State the phylum and class of specimen A."
2 marks

Step 5 — Comparison

"Tabulate THREE differences between specimens A and K."
3–6 marks (must be in table)

Step 6 — Adaptation

"State TWO adaptive features of specimen J for its habitat."
2–4 marks

Step 7 — Drawing

"Make a labelled drawing 6–8 cm long of specimen F."
6–10 marks

Step 8 — Ecology

"Name the type of relationship shown in specimen G."
1–2 marks

How WAEC Chooses Specimens Each Year

The Selection System

  • WAEC examiners work from the official WAEC Biology syllabus — every specimen must appear in the syllabus topics
  • Specimens are selected to cover at least 3 of the 5 biological kingdoms (Plants, Animals, Fungi, etc.)
  • At least one specimen must allow a biological drawing question
  • At least one specimen must allow a comparison/tabulation question
  • Questions must test across different cognitive levels: recall, application, analysis
  • Specimens are chosen so schools in all WAEC countries (Nigeria, Ghana, Sierra Leone, etc.) can source them locally
  • Examiners deliberately mix plant and animal specimens to prevent narrow preparation
  • Food test specimens appear almost every year — always prepare them

🎯 Examiner Pattern: What Repeats Every Year

Near-certain topics: Seeds (monocot vs dicot), Fish adaptive features, Food tests (starch/protein/fat), Ecological relationships (parasitism/mutualism), Reproductive structures (flower/fruit), Insect external features, Alimentary canal/digestive organs.

Q: State the food test used to detect lipids in Specimen B (crushed groundnut) and state the expected result.
Test: Grease spot test — rub Specimen B on a piece of white paper and hold to light.
Expected result: A permanent, translucent (greasy) stain is formed on the paper.
Alternative test: Dissolve in ethanol, pour into water — milky white emulsion forms.

Q: Name the reagent used to test for protein in Specimen H (evaporated milk) and state the expected result.
Reagent: Biuret reagent (sodium hydroxide solution + copper sulphate solution).
Expected result: A violet / purple colour appears.
[ 2 MARKS — 1 for reagent, 1 for result ]

Food class: Carbohydrate (starch).
Test: Iodine solution test — add 2–3 drops of iodine solution to maize flour or cut surface.
Expected result: Blue-black coloration.

Examination TechniqueWAEC Biological Drawing Rules

The 8 Laws of WAEC Drawing

  • Use 2B pencil only — never pen or coloured pencil for the diagram itself
  • Draw in the centre of the allocated space, 6–8 cm long (unless stated otherwise)
  • Single, clean lines — no sketching, shading, or artistic effects
  • Labels on horizontal lines drawn with a ruler, pointing to the correct structure with a straight line. Lines must NOT cross each other
  • Title below the diagram in full — e.g., "Diagram of the External Features of Specimen J (Fish)"
  • State magnification below the title — e.g., "Magnification: ×1" (actual size) or use the formula: Drawing size ÷ Actual size
  • No freehand label lines — use a ruler for all leader lines
  • Proportions must be correct — the drawing must look like the real specimen

🖊️ How Examiners Mark Drawings

Typically 6–10 marks for a drawing question. Marks are given for: (1) correct outline/shape, (2) correct proportions, (3) each correct label — usually 1 mark per label, up to 4–6 labels required, (4) title, (5) magnification. You can lose 2–3 marks just from missing the title and magnification — always write them.

Q: Make a drawing 6–8 cm long of Specimen J (fresh fish) and label FOUR external features.
Required labels (any 4): Dorsal fin, Caudal fin (tail fin), Pectoral fin, Pelvic fin, Anal fin, Scales, Eye, Mouth, Operculum (gill cover), Lateral line.

Title: Diagram of the External Features of Specimen J (Fresh Fish)
Magnification: ×1 (or measured ratio)

Mark DefenceCommon Mistakes That Lose Marks

❌ Prose Comparison

Writing "Groundnut has 2 cotyledons while maize has 1" in a paragraph instead of a table. Costs –1 mark automatically.

❌ No Magnification

Forgetting to write "Magnification: ×1" under your drawing. Costs 1 mark every time.

❌ No Drawing Title

Not writing the full title under the diagram. "Diagram of Fish" is wrong. Must include Specimen letter and full name.

❌ Vague Features

"It has a body" / "It is found in water" — these earn 0 marks. Features must be specific and observable.

❌ Wrong Reagent Name

Saying "iodine" for protein test instead of "Biuret reagent." One wrong reagent = 0 marks for the whole food test.

❌ Crossing Label Lines

Drawing labels that cross each other on a diagram. This costs you labelling marks. Plan label positions before drawing.

❌ Wrong Cotyledon

Calling maize "dicotyledonous" or groundnut "monocotyledonous." This is a major error — always confirmed by leaf venation too.

❌ Incomplete Adaptations

Saying "fish has fins" without explaining how: "Fins enable movement and steering through water." Feature + function = full mark.

Golden Rule for Observable Features: Only write what you can SEE, FEEL, or SMELL with the specimen in front of you. Do not write information from memory if you cannot observe it directly (e.g., don't write "contains vitamin C" for tomato — you cannot observe this).

Dicot vs Monocot Quick Check

2 = DICOT · 1 = MONOCOT

2 cotyledons = Dicot (groundnut, bean, tomato)
1 cotyledon = Monocot (maize, grasses)
Dicots: NET venation, TAPROOT, BROAD leaves
Monocots: PARALLEL venation, FIBROUS root, NARROW leaves
